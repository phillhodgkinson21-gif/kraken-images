# Kinetic Stencil

**A visual philosophy: letters are not printed on the picture — they are cut through it.**

Every composition begins with darkness. A field of near-black footage, drained of colour, sits at the back like soot on steel — still moving, still breathing, but held down to a whisper. Across it, a few enormous condensed words are carved as if by a plasma cutter, and through those cuts the world bursts back at full voltage: mud, chrome, smoke, sky, stage light. Type stops being a label laid over an image and becomes the only window into it. The eye reads the word and watches the motion in the same instant. Nothing is pasted on; everything is revealed.

Space is architectural and severe. The headline occupies the centre mass of a tall vertical frame, stacked word over word with tight leading, set so large that letterforms nearly touch the margins — monumental, load-bearing, Brutalist in weight. Around it, generous dark margins act as the frame's bodywork. The top and bottom edges carry a thin instrumentation layer: monospaced coordinates, hairline rules, registration crosshairs in the corners, a catalogue index. It reads like a technical plate from a workshop manual for an imaginary machine — the precision of an engineer's drawing applied to raw spectacle. Every margin, every baseline, every hairline offset is meticulously calibrated, the product of painstaking, master-level adjustment.

Colour is rationed. Near-black ground. One electric cyan for hairlines and a single kicker line. One burnt gold, used once, for the date. Everything else is the footage itself — the only full-colour element in the frame is what lives inside the letters, so the type becomes the brightest, most saturated thing anywhere. Restraint is the point: a limited palette makes the windows of motion feel like they are glowing from within.

Rhythm is mechanical and patient. Words do not bounce or spin; they are machined open, one line after another, with a short wipe like a shutter or a roller door lifting — then they hold, steady, letting the footage do the moving. Small labels arrive after the headline settles, quietly. Long holds reward the viewer. There is no gimmickry, no glow filters, no stock transitions — only the cut, the reveal and the stillness after it. Each frame should look like it was storyboarded, timed and re-timed by someone at the very top of motion design.

Typography is a dialogue between two voices: a towering condensed grotesque that shouts, and a small monospaced instrument face that annotates. Text is sparse — a headline of two to four words, a handful of labels — never sentences. Hierarchy is enforced by scale alone: the gulf between the giant cut-out word and the tiny mono notation is the whole system. Nothing overlaps, nothing crowds an edge, nothing is decorative. The finished piece must feel hand-built, obsessively refined and unmistakably human — a meticulously crafted artifact, not a template.

## Addendum — The Clean Plate

Some images are too ornate to be cut. A dense, saturated scene — towering forms, glowing light, layered detail — fights the stencil: the letters dissolve into it and the word is lost. Such an image is never used as fill. Instead the stencil is allowed to finish its sentence, and then the shutter closes: a breath of pure ink, a quarter-second of nothing. When it opens, the image arrives whole, uncut and full-bleed, given the entire frame like a gallery wall given to a single canvas. Only the instrumentation survives — the hairlines, the coordinates, the small cyan label naming what we are looking at — held in place by a soft fall of shadow at the head and foot of the frame so they never compete with the picture. The contrast between the cut and the clean plate is the composition: first the word, then the world. Its timing is measured to the frame and refined until the cut feels inevitable, the work of painstaking, master-level attention.

The foot of every frame is a fixed architecture of gratitude: the date in gold, the paired marks of the cause beside it, and beneath, a single quiet line of partners rendered as pure white silhouettes — equal in height, equal in weight, set with the same exacting spacing as everything above them.
